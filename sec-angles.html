    Section 1.3 Angles

    Definition 1.3.1.

    A subset of the plane is convex if it contains the whole segment that connects any two of its points.
    🔗
    🔗
    For example, one side of any line is a convex set, and triangles are convex sets.
    🔗
    We do not define the term angle formally. Instead we say: There are things called angles. To each angle is associated:
    1. a unique point \(A\text{,}\) called its vertex;
      🔗
      🔗
    2. two rays \([AB\) and \([AC\text{,}\) both starting at the vertex, and called the arms of the angle;
      🔗
      🔗
    3. a piece of the plane called the inside of the angle.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    An angle is either a null angle, an ordinary angle, a straight angle, a reflex angle or a full angle. Unless otherwise specified, you may take it that any angle we talk about is an ordinary angle.
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.2.

    An angle is a null angle if its arms coincide with one another and its inside is the empty set.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.3.

    An angle is an ordinary angle if its arms are not on one line, and its inside is a convex set.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.4.

    An angle is a straight angle if its arms are the two halves of one line, and its inside is one of the sides of that line.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.5.

    An angle is a reflex angle if its arms are not on one line, and its inside is not a convex set.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.6.

    An angle is a full angle if its arms coincide with one another and its inside is the rest of the plane.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.7.

    Suppose that \(A\text{,}\) \(B\text{,}\) and \(C\) are three noncollinear points. We denote the (ordinary) angle with arms \([AB\) and \([AC\) by \(\angle BAC\) (and also by \(\angle CAB\)). We shall also use the notation \(\angle BAC\) to refer to straight angles, where \(A\text{,}\) \(B\text{,}\) \(C\) are collinear, and \(A\) lies between \(B\) and \(C\) (either side could be the inside of this angle).
    🔗
    Sometimes we want to refer to an angle without naming points, and in that case we use lower-case Greek letters, \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\text{,}\) etc.
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises Exercises

    1.

    An angle has arms that coincide with one another and its inside is the empty set. What type of angle is it?
    🔗
    Hint.
    Review the definitions of the five types of angle.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    A null angle.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    By definition, a null angle is one whose arms coincide with one another and whose inside is the empty set. This matches the description exactly.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    For the angle \(\angle BAC\text{,}\) identify the vertex and the two arms.
    🔗
    Hint.
    The vertex is the unique point associated with the angle — the common starting point of the two rays.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    Vertex: \(A\text{.}\) Arms: \([AB\) and \([AC\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    The angle \(\angle BAC\) has vertex \(A\text{.}\) The two arms are the rays \([AB\) and \([AC\text{,}\) both starting at \(A\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Is a line segment \([AB]\) a convex set? Explain your answer.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Pick any two points on the segment. Does the segment connecting them stay inside \([AB]\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    Yes, a line segment is a convex set.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    A set is convex if it contains the whole segment connecting any two of its points. If we pick any two points on \([AB]\text{,}\) the segment between them lies entirely within \([AB]\text{.}\) So \([AB]\) is a convex set.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    What is the key difference between an ordinary angle and a straight angle?
    🔗
    Hint.
    Think about the position of the arms in each case.
    🔗
    🔗
    Answer.
    In an ordinary angle the arms are not on one line; in a straight angle the arms are the two halves of one line.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    An ordinary angle has arms that are not on one line, and its inside is a convex set. A straight angle has arms that form the two halves of a single line (pointing in opposite directions), and its inside is one of the sides of that line.
    🔗
